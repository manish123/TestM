# What is Tithi?

Tithi is a traditional lunar day used in the Hindu lunisolar calendar. It is not simply the same thing as a civil calendar date or a fixed 24-hour period.

A Tithi is defined by the changing angular separation between the Moon and the Sun. As that separation increases, successive Tithis are completed. There are 30 Tithis in a lunar month, divided into two 15-Tithi halves: Shukla Paksha, the waxing half, and Krishna Paksha, the waning half.

## Why does Tithi exist?

The Tithi provides a way of organizing the lunar month according to the relative positions of the Sun and Moon. It is fundamental to many Hindu calendrical rules because numerous festivals, Vratas and observances are defined by a particular Tithi or by the relationship of a Tithi to sunrise or another specified time.

This is why a Tithi does not map one-to-one onto a civil date. A Tithi can begin during one civil day and end during another, and a civil day can contain more than one Tithi boundary.

## How is Tithi calculated?

The basic calculation uses the geocentric ecliptic longitudes of the Sun and Moon. Their angular separation is taken modulo 360 degrees.

A new Tithi begins whenever the separation advances by another 12 degrees:

- 0°–12°: first Tithi
- 12°–24°: second Tithi
- and so on through 360°

Because the Moon's apparent motion relative to the Sun is not uniform over a civil day, Tithis do not all have the same duration. A Tithi may last substantially less or more than 24 hours.

The astronomical event is global, but the displayed civil date and clock time depend on the chosen location and time zone. Traditional observance rules can additionally specify how the Tithi is evaluated relative to sunrise or another local boundary.

## Traditional interpretation

Tithi is one of the five principal limbs of Panchang. Specific Tithis are associated with particular festivals and Vratas, such as Ekadashi, Purnima and Amavasya.

Traditional practice does not treat all Tithis as interchangeable. Different Tithis have different ritual associations, and particular observances are determined using rules that can involve the exact timing of the Tithi and the local sunrise.

The interpretation here is calendrical and traditional. It should not be presented as a scientific claim that a Tithi independently causes a particular outcome.

## How should Tithi be used?

For practical Panchang use, first identify the Tithi and its exact beginning and ending time for your location. Then apply the rule relevant to the activity or observance you are considering.

For example, if an observance is defined by a particular Tithi being present at sunrise, simply knowing that the Tithi occurs somewhere during the civil date is not enough. The local Panchang calculation must be checked.

This is one reason Panchang is more useful as a decision-supporting time system than as a simple list of dates.

## Common misconceptions

### Tithi means the same thing as a calendar date

No. A Tithi is a lunar calendrical interval defined astronomically. A civil date is a local 24-hour calendar day.

### Every Tithi lasts exactly one day

No. Tithi duration varies because the relative angular motion of the Moon and Sun varies.

### A Tithi always begins at sunrise

No. A Tithi can begin or end at any local clock time. Sunrise becomes important when a particular observance rule specifies how the Tithi is to be evaluated.

### Purnima and Amavasya are just full moon and new moon as visual events

They are related to the lunar phase, but the calendrical definitions and observance rules are expressed through Tithi and the relevant angular relationship, not simply through visual appearance.
